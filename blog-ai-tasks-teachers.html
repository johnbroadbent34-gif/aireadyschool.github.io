Workload

5 tasks teachers can hand to AI today — without the guilt

JD
John
Deputy Headteacher & Founder
6 min read
November 2025
Workload & Practice

Teacher workload is one of the most serious challenges facing the profession. AI, used properly and within approved tools, can meaningfully reduce it. Here are five places to start — with no data risks, no ethical grey areas, and no need to feel like you're cutting corners.

There is a hesitation many teachers feel around using AI for their work. A sense that it might be cheating somehow — that the hours spent crafting lesson plans, writing reports, and preparing resources are part of the job in a way that can't simply be handed off.

That hesitation is worth examining. Teaching is a professional practice. Professionals use tools. The question is never whether to use tools — it is which tools, in what contexts, with what level of oversight. A teacher who uses AI to draft a first version of a letter to parents is no different from a teacher who uses a template, or asks a colleague to cast an eye over a resource before using it.

What matters is that the judgement, the professional knowledge, and the relationship with pupils remains yours. AI handles the scaffolding. You handle the substance.

With that in mind — and assuming you are using an approved tool within your school's data tenancy — here are five tasks where AI genuinely earns its place.

Before you start

All five tasks below assume you are using an AI tool approved by your school — one that sits within your Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace tenancy, or has been assessed by your DPO. Never enter pupil names, personal data, or sensitive information into any AI tool. The tasks below are chosen specifically because they don't require you to.

01
Planning
First drafts of lesson plans and sequences
AI is well-suited to producing a structured first draft of a lesson plan or a short sequence of lessons. Give it your year group, topic, curriculum objective, and any specific constraints — and it will return a workable scaffold in seconds. You then apply your knowledge of your class, your school's approach, and your professional judgement to shape it into something genuinely useful.
Example prompt
"Create a sequence of 3 lessons for Year 5 on place value, aligned to the KS2 maths curriculum. Each lesson should include a starter, main activity with differentiation ideas, and a plenary. No pupil names or data needed."
⚠
Always review before using. AI lesson plans can miss the nuances of your class, your school's sequencing, or recent learning. Treat every output as a draft, not a finished product.
02
Communication
Parent and carer communications
Letters, emails, and newsletter contributions take up a disproportionate amount of teacher and leader time — particularly when the message is sensitive or needs careful framing. AI can draft a clear, professional first version in the right tone. You review, personalise where needed, and send. Time saved: significant. Professional quality: maintained.
Example prompt
"Write a friendly but professional email to parents explaining a change to the school's homework policy. The change is moving from weekly written homework to reading-focused homework. Tone should be warm and reassuring."
⚠
Never include pupil names or personal details in your prompt. Keep prompts generic and personalise the output yourself before sending.
03
Assessment
Generating success criteria and mark schemes
Writing clear success criteria and mark schemes is one of those tasks that is important, time-consuming, and largely formulaic once you know what you're assessing. AI can generate a solid first version based on your assessment objective, key stage, and the specific task — leaving you to refine the language and ensure it matches your school's assessment approach.
Example prompt
"Create a 4-level mark scheme for a KS3 persuasive writing task. The levels should be: emerging, developing, secure, and mastery. Focus on structure, language choices, and rhetorical techniques."
⚠
Check alignment with your school's policy. AI-generated mark schemes need to match your school's assessment language and grade descriptors — don't assume they will by default.
04
CPD & Admin
Summarising documents and research
Teachers and leaders are sent an enormous volume of reading — new guidance, research summaries, policy documents, inspection frameworks. AI can read and summarise long documents quickly, pulling out the key points most relevant to your context. This is particularly useful for keeping up with DfE guidance, Ofsted frameworks, and subject association updates without spending an hour on each one.
Example prompt
"Summarise the key changes in this document relevant to a primary school leader. Focus on anything that requires a policy change or staff action. Present as a bullet-pointed briefing I can share with SLT." [paste document text]
⚠
Verify important details. AI summaries can miss nuance or misrepresent specific requirements. Always check the original source for anything that will inform a policy or governance decision.
05
Resources
Creating differentiated resource variations
One of the most time-consuming aspects of lesson preparation is creating multiple versions of the same resource at different levels. AI can take a base resource — a reading passage, a set of questions, a task brief — and produce adapted versions at different reading ages or challenge levels in moments. You check them, adjust the language where needed, and have a differentiated set in a fraction of the usual time.
Example prompt
"Take this passage about the water cycle and create two versions: one simplified for readers working at a Year 3 level, and one extended challenge version for Year 6 readers. Maintain the key scientific vocabulary in both." [paste passage]
⚠
Read every output carefully. AI-simplified text sometimes loses important meaning or introduces inaccuracies. Your subject knowledge is the quality check here.

The ground rules that make this work safely

Non-negotiables for using AI in your professional practice
1
Use approved tools only. Only use AI tools that your school has sanctioned — within your data tenancy and assessed by your DPO. Consumer tools like the free version of ChatGPT are not appropriate for school tasks.
2
Never enter pupil data. No names, no SEND information, no safeguarding details, no assessment data. Keep every prompt generic. The AI does not need to know who your pupils are to be useful.
3
You are always the professional. Every AI output should be treated as a first draft written by a capable but uninformed assistant. Your knowledge, your judgement, and your accountability remain yours entirely.
4
Check outputs before using them. AI can be confidently wrong. Factual errors, outdated references, and misaligned content are all possible. Read everything before it reaches pupils or parents.
5
Be transparent with your school. If your school doesn't have a clear AI policy yet, raise it. The best time to establish shared expectations around AI use is before an issue arises, not after.
The bigger point

Using AI for administrative and preparatory tasks is not laziness — it is professional efficiency. The goal is not to do less teaching. It is to spend more of your finite time and energy on the parts of the job that genuinely require a human: relationships, responsiveness, and the thousand daily judgements that define great teaching.

The guilt, when it comes, is usually a sign that you care about doing the job properly. That instinct is worth protecting. The task now is pointing it in the right direction — towards the quality of what reaches your pupils, not towards the number of hours you spent producing it.
