Leadership

Why your AI strategy needs to start with your staff, not your software

JD
John
Deputy Headteacher & Founder
8 min read
October 2025
Leadership & Strategy

Most school AI rollouts follow the same pattern — a tool gets chosen, a training session gets booked, and then nothing much changes. The reason is almost always the same: the software came before the people. Here is how to flip that, and why it matters.

There is a pattern that plays out in schools across the country right now. A headteacher reads about AI, attends a conference, or gets a compelling demonstration from an EdTech vendor. Excited by the possibilities, they purchase a tool, arrange a staff training afternoon, and wait for the transformation to begin.

Six months later, a handful of staff are using it enthusiastically, most have quietly reverted to their previous ways of working, and the tool sits largely unused on the school's system. Sound familiar?

This isn't a failure of will or even of training. It is a failure of sequence. The software came before the people — and that order makes sustainable change almost impossible.

Why software-first approaches fail

When a new tool arrives before staff understand the landscape it sits within, it creates several predictable problems. First, staff have no framework for evaluating it. Without a grounded understanding of what AI is, how it works, and where it sits in relation to their professional practice, staff can't make informed decisions about when and how to use it. They either over-rely on it — accepting outputs uncritically — or reject it entirely out of uncertainty.

Second, concerns go underground. Staff who have worries about AI — ethical concerns, workload fears, questions about pupil data — don't have a language or a safe space to raise them. Those concerns don't disappear; they become passive resistance to the whole initiative.

Third, and most damagingly, the message it sends is wrong. Leading with software implies that the technology is the point. It positions staff as users of a vendor's product rather than as professionals making considered judgements about tools in their practice.

The pattern to avoid

Tool purchase → Training session → Assume adoption. This sequence treats AI implementation like an IT rollout. It isn't. It is a cultural and professional change that requires the same care as any other significant shift in school practice.

What staff-first actually means

A staff-first approach does not mean delaying tool use indefinitely or making AI adoption contingent on everyone feeling enthusiastic about it. It means ensuring that the professional understanding comes before — or at the very least alongside — the practical application.

Specifically, it means three things.

1
Build shared understanding before anything else
Every member of staff — teachers, support staff, administrators — should have a genuine, grounded understanding of what generative AI is and what it is not. Not a vendor demo. Not a show-and-tell. A structured opportunity to ask questions, express concerns, and develop a shared language around AI before any tool is introduced.
2
Make the risks visible and discussable
Staff are not best served by being sold the upside of AI without an honest account of the risks. Data privacy, hallucination, bias, academic integrity, safeguarding implications — these need to be on the table from the start. Staff who understand the risks make better decisions. Staff who discover risks after the fact lose trust in leadership.
3
Invite staff into the decision-making
The best AI implementations in schools have involved staff in tool selection, policy development, and the identification of genuine use cases. This does not mean decision by committee. It means leaders making informed choices with meaningful staff input — and being transparent about the reasoning.

The trust problem at the heart of AI adoption

Underneath most failed AI rollouts is a trust problem. Staff don't trust that the tool has been properly vetted. They don't trust that their concerns have been heard. And in many cases — particularly in schools that have moved fast — they don't trust that their data, or their pupils' data, is being handled appropriately.

Trust is not built through a training session. It is built through transparency, through time, and through a consistent demonstration that leadership has thought carefully about what they are asking staff to engage with.

"A staff member who understands AI — genuinely understands it — is more valuable than any AI tool you will ever deploy. Because they will use every tool better, question outputs more rigorously, and protect your pupils more effectively."

— John, AI Ready Schools

What this looks like in practice

A staff-first approach does not require a lengthy delay before any AI is used in school. It requires a deliberate investment — of time, leadership attention, and honesty — in the human side of the change before or alongside the technical side.

In practical terms, that means starting the year with a structured conversation about AI rather than a tool demonstration. It means creating space in a staff meeting — not an INSET day devoted to a vendor product, but an honest discussion — for staff to ask: what is this, what does it mean for my role, and what does our school believe about using it?

It means your policy existing before your tool list, not after it. It means your DSL and DPO being involved from the start, not consulted after the fact. And it means your governing body understanding the approach well enough to ask good questions, not just rubber-stamp a purchase.

A practical starting point

Before your next AI-related decision — whether that's a tool purchase, a policy update, or a training session — ask: do our staff have a shared, grounded understanding of what AI is and what it isn't? If the honest answer is no, that is where to start.

The principles that make it work

Understanding before tools
Build staff knowledge of AI concepts before introducing any specific product. The understanding is transferable; the tool will change.
Honesty about risks
Be transparent about what AI cannot do, where it fails, and what the safeguarding and data implications are. This builds trust, not fear.
Policy before procurement
Your school's values and boundaries around AI use should be articulated before a tool is purchased — not shaped around vendor capabilities.
Sustained CPD not one-off training
AI is developing faster than any single training session can address. Build a culture of ongoing learning, not a one-time event.
Staff voice in implementation
The people closest to pupils and classroom practice will identify the most valuable and the most risky use cases. Involve them.
Measured pace
Sustainable AI adoption in schools is not fast. It is consistent. Small steps, embedded well, will always outperform ambitious rollouts that lose momentum.

The bigger picture

The schools that will navigate AI best over the next decade are not necessarily the ones that adopt it first. They are the ones that build genuine staff capability and a strong ethical framework — and then apply it consistently as the technology evolves.

That is the investment worth making. Not in any single tool, but in the professional understanding and institutional confidence that makes every tool your school ever uses safer, smarter, and more sustainable.

Start with your staff. The software will follow.
